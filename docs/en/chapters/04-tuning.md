# Chapter 4 · Tuning Systems as an Optimization Problem

<div class="chapter-meta">
  <span>Estimated 50 minutes</span>
  <span>Prerequisite: Chapters 2 and 3</span>
  <span>Code: code/ch04_tuning.py</span>
</div>

This chapter answers the following questions:

1. Why does going 12 steps around the circle of fifths fail to return to the starting point? Where does the missing bit go?
2. Why do violinists prefer perfect fifths when tuning, while piano makers build only 12-TET?
3. The just major third sounds purest; why can't all 12 keys be tuned in just intonation?
4. Almost every interval in 12-TET is an irrational ratio. Does that count as "out of tune"?
5. If we are not restricted to 12 notes, is there a more accurate division?

Chapter 2 gave us pitch coordinates and Chapter 3 a roughness model. Now switch to an engineering question: **given a set of simple integer ratios we want to approximate, how should the notes within an octave be arranged so that both error and playing cost are acceptable?** This chapter first compares how Pythagorean tuning and just intonation distribute error, then adds the constraint that "after transposition by any step, the pitch-class set still lands on the original grid," derives equal temperament, and finally lets the number of pitch classes $n$ vary as well. Here "error" is always relative to a specified target; deviating from 12-TET is not in itself out of tune.

## 4.1 The Pythagorean Predicament: Pythagorean Tuning and the Wolf Interval

### 4.1.1 The Chain of Fifths: Building a Scale with the Simplest Operation

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 4.1 (tuning system)">

A **tuning system** is a set of rules that assigns each pitch class a frequency relative to a reference. If the rules are generated by repeatedly stacking finitely many frequency ratios, it is called a **generated tuning**. If a tuning uses a fixed interval as its step and maps the pitch-class grid back onto itself (modulo the octave) under transposition by any integer number of steps, it is called an **equal temperament**. Note: multiplying any set of notes by the same frequency ratio preserves the intervals within the set; what equal temperament additionally requires is that the **grid be closed under transposition**.

</Box>

The simplest generated tuning uses only two operations: multiply by $3/2$ (go up a perfect fifth), and "divide by 2 whenever you exceed an octave" (fold back). Starting from C:

$$
\mathrm{C} \xrightarrow{\times 3/2} \mathrm{G} \xrightarrow{\times 3/2} \mathrm{D} \xrightarrow{\times 3/2} \mathrm{A} \xrightarrow{\times 3/2} \mathrm{E} \cdots
$$

Each step adds $701.955$ ¢ to the pitch coordinate and subtracts $1200$ whenever it exceeds $1200$ ¢. That is the entire algorithm of **Pythagorean tuning**. It is simple enough to do in your head and old enough to be traced back to before the Common Era.

Table 1 of Experiment 4.1 runs this process for 12 steps:

| Step $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | … | 11 | 12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cumulative ¢ | 0 | 701.955 | 1403.910 | … | 7721.505 | 8423.460 |
| Folded ¢ | 0 | 701.955 | 203.910 | … | 521.505 | 23.460 |

Step 12 does not return to 0 but lands at $23.460$ ¢. In other words, **12 perfect fifths do not equal 7 octaves**. This discrepancy has a name of its own.

### 4.1.2 The Comma: A Gap That Cannot Be Eliminated

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 4.2 (Pythagorean comma)">

$$
\text{comma} = 1200\left(12\log_2\frac{3}{2} - 7\right) \approx 23.460\ \text{¢}.
$$
It equals the interval corresponding to the ratio of $(3/2)^{12}$ to $2^{7}$. That ratio itself is
$$
\frac{(3/2)^{12}}{2^{7}} = \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} = \frac{531441}{524288} \approx 1.013643.
$$

</Box>

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 4.1 (the chain of fifths cannot close exactly)">

There is no integer $m$ such that $(3/2)^{12} = 2^{m}$. Equivalently, there are no positive integers $a, b$ such that $3^{a} = 2^{b}$.

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

If $a, b \ge 1$, the left side $3^{a}$ is odd and the right side $2^{b}$ is even, a contradiction. So there is no positive integer solution. Back to the chain of fifths: $(3/2)^{12} > 1$, so if it equaled $2^{m}$, we would have $m \ge 0$, hence $3^{12} = 2^{12+m}$. The left side is odd and the right side is even (since $12 + m \ge 1$), a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

This proof uses only one fact: **unique prime factorization**. $3^{12}$ contains no factor 2 and $2^{19}$ contains no factor 3, so the two cannot be equal. Thus "the chain of fifths returns to the start after 12 steps" is not a precision problem but a number-theoretic impossibility. No matter how many decimal places you measure frequencies to, the gap is there.

Incidentally: 12 fifths are $8423.460$ ¢ and 7 octaves are $8400$ ¢, a difference of $23.460$ ¢. This difference sounds small — about a quarter of a semitone — but it is enough for a note to drift "further and further" along the chain of fifths until the ear can hear it.

### 4.1.3 The Wolf Fifth: Where the Gap Lands

After the 12 fifth-generated pitch classes are folded into one octave, they are not evenly distributed. Table 3 of Experiment 4.1 lists their spacings; there are only two values:

- $90.225$ ¢, called the **limma**, the natural semitone of Pythagorean tuning, e.g. E–F;
- $113.685$ ¢, called the **apotome**, the chromatic semitone, e.g. C–C♯.

The apotome is **not a major second**: the Pythagorean whole tone $9:8$ is $203.910$ ¢. The two semitones differ by exactly $23.460$ ¢ — the comma again. Worse, between the 11th step's pitch class ($521.505$ ¢) and the next C there remains only

$$
1200 - 521.505 = 678.495\ \text{¢},
$$

shorter than a perfect fifth by $701.955 - 678.495 = 23.460$ ¢. This severely narrow fifth is the **wolf fifth**. Its harmonics cannot align the way a perfect fifth's do, and it stands out especially in harmony. If you insist on a closed 12-note chain of fifths with the other 11 fifths pure, this gap can only be moved, never removed.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: the wolf interval is 'bad tuning'">

No. Under the constraints above — 12 notes, a closed octave, and the other 11 fifths all $3:2$ — the wolf interval is a number-theoretic gap, not an operational mistake; the closing fifth must be $678.495$ ¢. If you allow an open chain, more pitch classes, or adjustments to several fifths, other temperaments are of course available; the claim here concerns this particular closed design, not that every Pythagorean scale has only this one wolf layout.

</Box>

### 4.1.4 Two Equivalent Ways to Generate It

At this point we can switch viewpoints. The chain of fifths has two formulations:

1. **Sequential folding**: start from C, multiply by $3/2$ each time, divide by $2$ if the result exceeds $2$, and record the result in the current octave. This is what the experiment script does.
2. **Walking on a circle**: view pitch-class space as a circle of circumference $1200$, take a step of $701.955$ ¢ each time, and reduce the position modulo $1200$. After 12 steps the total distance is $8423.460 = 7 \times 1200 + 23.460$, so the endpoint is $23.460$ ¢ away from the start.

The two formulations are mathematically equivalent, because "folding back" is reduction modulo. The difference is only in intuition: sequential folding shows you "history"; walking on a circle shows you "geometry." The ratio of step to circumference is $\log_2(3/2)$; it is irrational, so there are no positive integers $a,b$ making $a$ perfect fifths exactly equal $b$ octaves. Step 12 is merely the most commonly used approximate closure point: by then the pitch-class circle has been traversed 7 times, plus another $23.460$ ¢.

## 4.2 The Local Optimum of Just Intonation: The Harmony Trap of Small Integer Ratios

### 4.2.1 5-Limit Just Intonation

Since the chain of fifths leaves a wolf, another idea is: **specify the frequency ratio of each pitch class directly, so that the most important intervals all take simple integer ratios.** Ratios using only the prime factors $2, 3, 5$ are called **5-limit** just intonation. The natural major scale on C is:

$$
1,\ \frac{9}{8},\ \frac{5}{4},\ \frac{4}{3},\ \frac{3}{2},\ \frac{5}{3},\ \frac{15}{8}.
$$

Compare Table 2 of Experiment 4.1; the deviations of the three systems are immediately visible (a positive sign means higher than 12-TET):

| Degree | 12-TET ¢ | Just ¢ | Just deviation ¢ | Pythagorean ¢ | Pythagorean deviation ¢ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 0 | 0.000 | 0 | 0.000 | 0 |
| D | 200 | 203.910 | +3.910 | 203.910 | +3.910 |
| E | 400 | 386.314 | −13.686 | 407.820 | +7.820 |
| F | 500 | 498.045 | −1.955 | 498.045 | −1.955 |
| G | 700 | 701.955 | +1.955 | 701.955 | +1.955 |
| A | 900 | 884.359 | −15.641 | 905.865 | +5.865 |
| B | 1100 | 1088.269 | −11.731 | 1109.775 | +9.775 |

The just major third $5:4 = 386.314$ ¢ is $13.686$ ¢ narrower than 12-TET, while the fifth differs by only $1.955$ ¢. This is exactly just intonation's selling point: **it tunes the thirds and fifths that are easiest to hear to exact simple ratios**. The roughness model of Chapter 3 supports this — the just major third has $D = 0.046$ ($S = 0.956$), while the 12-TET major third has $S = 0.940$; the fifth has $S = 1.000$ under both systems.

So in a fixed key, just intonation is a **local optimum**: given the tonic, it places the tonic, dominant, and subdominant chords all at their most consonant positions.

### 4.2.2 The Problem of Three Major Thirds

Why can't the local optimum be promoted to a global one? First, a number-theoretic fact. Stack three just major thirds:

$$
3 \times 386.314 = 1158.941\ \text{¢}.
$$

An octave is $1200$ ¢, so there remains

$$
1200 - 1158.941 = 41.059\ \text{¢}.
$$

This difference is called the **greater diesis**. It means: you cannot have C–E, E–G♯, and G♯–C all equal to $5:4$ at the same time. If the first two are exact, the third must be $41.059$ ¢ short; if all three are exact, the octave must grow by $41.059$ ¢, and it is no longer $2:1$.

The same contradiction keeps appearing as you change keys. With C major in just intonation, requiring D–F♯ to also be $5:4$ forces

$$
F^\sharp=D\times\frac54=\frac98\times\frac54=\frac{45}{32},
$$

i.e. $590.224$ ¢. It is not among the seven diatonic degrees of C major, nor is it equal to the F♯ at $611.730$ ¢ on the chain of fifths. If you use the latter instead, D–F♯ becomes $81:64=407.820$ ¢, **and the just major third is not preserved**. One key must serve several keys, and the conflict follows.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: just intonation is a 'more accurate 12-TET'">

Just intonation is not a precision upgrade of 12-TET but **a different set of targets**. In Table 2, A is $15.641$ ¢ lower than 12-TET, precisely because the just major sixth is $5:3$; a deviation from equal temperament is not an intrinsic tuning error.

The real cost has to be seen in the interval network. For example, in the C-major just tuning above, D–A has ratio $(5/3)/(9/8)=40/27$, only $680.449$ ¢, not a $3:2$ perfect fifth. Retuning D–A to a perfect fifth would in turn disturb other intervals. "Local optimum" means protecting the chosen tonic, dominant, and subdominant chords first, not making every key and every fifth optimal at once.

</Box>

### 4.2.3 Local Optima, Global Collapse

Put the two facts together: a just-intonation tuning chosen around C major can protect the three triads on C, F, and G first; but when the music modulates to D major or elsewhere, the same fixed set of pitch classes may no longer supply the same simple ratios. Real fixed-pitch instruments cannot "change strings for every key":

- each of the piano's 88 keys has only one string (or one group), and one tuning must serve all keys;
- guitar frets are fixed; changing key only changes where you press;
- the standing wave in a wind instrument is set by tube length, so the scale structure cannot be changed on the fly.

Just intonation and Pythagorean tuning can of course also be installed on fixed instruments; different keys simply present different interval qualities. If you further require that **transposition by any semitone still uses the same grid and the error of each named interval stays the same**, unequal 12-note schemes are excluded, leaving only equal temperament with equally spaced adjacent degrees. Section 4.3 proves that the non-octave intervals of this equally spaced grid must use irrational frequency ratios.

## 4.3 12-TET: The Compromise of Irrational Numbers

### 4.3.1 Definition and Irrationality

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 4.3 (n-tone equal temperament, n-TET)">

Divide the octave into $n$ equal parts; the frequency of the $k$-th degree is
$$
f_k = f_0 \cdot 2^{k/n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1.
$$
Adjacent degrees always have frequency ratio $q_n = 2^{1/n}$ and interval $1200/n$ ¢. For $n = 12$ this is 12-TET, with $q_{12} = 2^{1/12} \approx 1.059463094$.

</Box>

The beauty of 12-TET: it turns every interval into "a function of the number of semitones." A fifth is 7 semitones, a fourth 5, a major third 4 — so transposition only adds a constant to the pitch-class number, and all frequency ratios are automatically preserved. The price: apart from the octave, no interval is a rational ratio anymore.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 4.2 (equal-temperament ratios cannot all be rational)">

Let $n \ge 2$ and let $k$ be an integer with $0 \lt k \lt n$. Then $2^{k/n}$ is irrational.

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

Suppose $2^{k/n} = p/q$, where $p, q$ are positive integers in lowest terms. Raise both sides to the $n$-th power:
$$
p^{n} = 2^{k} q^{n}.
$$
The right side contains a factor 2, so $p^{n}$ is even, hence $p$ is even; write $p = 2r$. Substituting gives
$$
2^{n} r^{n} = 2^{k} q^{n}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q^{n} = 2^{n-k} r^{n}.
$$
Since $0 \lt k \lt n$, we have $n - k \ge 1$, so $q^{n}$ is even and $q$ is even. This contradicts $p, q$ being coprime. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

The irony of this theorem: **the "equal" in 12-TET is precisely what forces the ratios to be irrational**. If all ratios were rational, the ratio of adjacent degrees would have to be a rational $q$ with $q^{12} = 2$, which has no rational solution. To make 12 intervals exactly alike, you must accept irrational numbers. Chapter 3 said "only rational ratios give harmonic coincidence," so 12-TET carries the original sin of "harmonics that never line up" from birth.

### 4.3.2 The Error Budget

Compare 12-TET with just intonation item by item (data from Tables 2 and 5 of Experiment 4.1):

- Fifth: 12-TET is $700$ ¢, just is $701.955$ ¢, **$1.955$ ¢ short**;
- Fourth: 12-TET is $500$ ¢, just is $498.045$ ¢, **$1.955$ ¢ long** (fourth and fifth are complementary to $1200$, so the errors cancel exactly);
- Major third: 12-TET is $400$ ¢, just is $386.314$ ¢, **$13.686$ ¢ wide**;
- Minor third: 12-TET is $300$ ¢, just is $315.642$ ¢, **$15.642$ ¢ narrow**;
- Major sixth: 12-TET is $900$ ¢, just is $884.359$ ¢, **$15.641$ ¢ wide**;
- Major seventh: 12-TET is $1100$ ¢, just is $1088.269$ ¢, **$11.731$ ¢ wide**.

The fifth differs by less than $2$ ¢, but "a small difference" is not the same as inaudible: once coinciding harmonics become nearly coinciding, slow beats can still arise. The major third differs by nearly $14$ ¢, and its beats are often more obvious; how audible this is varies with register, timbre, loudness, and listening task, so no fixed cent threshold summarizes it. Equal temperament is still widely accepted, for three main reasons:

1. **The roughness cost is limited**: the harmonics of the 12-TET major third are **approximately** coincident rather than exactly coincident; in the register and spectrum chosen in Chapter 3, $S = 0.940$, only slightly below just intonation's $0.956$;
2. **The transposition benefit is huge**: all keys share one fingering and one instrument; the error is "paid once, enjoyed globally";
3. **Hearing and learning**: long exposure makes 12-TET intervals a familiar reference, and in some contexts a just major third may even sound narrow.

### 4.3.3 Why It Is an Engineering Victory

12-TET is not the "mathematical optimum" but **the least bad solution under constraints**. Its constraints are:

- only 12 notes per octave (limited by keyboard, fingering, memory);
- all keys share one set of frequencies (limited by instrument manufacturing);
- the frequency of each degree is determined solely by "the number of semitones" (limited by notation and transposition).

Under these three constraints, 12-TET is the only choice: divide into 12 equal parts, and there is no alternative. Its error distribution is quite uneven: the fifth is close to $3:2$, while the thirds make an obvious compromise. There is no need to attach the label "Pareto optimal" here — once "12 notes, equal division, closed under transposition" are all written as hard constraints, there is only one feasible solution to begin with; the real engineering judgment is whether to accept these constraints and their errors.

Section 4.4 relaxes the first constraint to see whether "more notes" can buy smaller errors.

## 4.4 Other Solution Spaces: Microtonality, Indian Tuning, and Arbitrary n

### 4.4.1 Treating n as a Variable

The $n$ in Definition 4.3 need not be 12. Taking $n = 19, 31, 41, 53$ gives 19-TET, 31-TET, and other "microtonal" systems. The question becomes: **given a set of target intervals and weights, which $n$ minimizes the weighted error?**

Experiment 4.1 performs a search with seven target intervals and weights set by hand to reflect target importance: perfect fifth $1.0$, major third $0.8$, minor third and major sixth $0.6$ each, major second $0.5$, minor second $0.3$, minor seventh $0.2$. The signed error is defined as "target cents minus the nearest integer multiple of $1200/n$," so a positive sign means the grid point is below the target; the weighted RMS is

$$
E(n) = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i w_i\, e_i(n)^2}{\sum_i w_i}}.
$$

Table 5 gives the scores of several candidates:

| $n$ | Cents per step | Fifth error ¢ | Major-third error ¢ | Max error ¢ | Weighted RMS ¢ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | 100.000 | +1.955 | −13.686 | 31.174 | 13.139 |
| 19 | 63.158 | +7.218 | +7.366 | 21.457 | 9.429 |
| 24 | 50.000 | +1.955 | −13.686 | 18.826 | 11.907 |
| 31 | 38.710 | +5.181 | −0.783 | 10.362 | 5.695 |
| 41 | 29.268 | −0.484 | +5.826 | 6.310 | 4.636 |
| 53 | 22.642 | +0.068 | +1.408 | 4.759 | 1.495 |

Within these 7 targets, this set of weights, and the search range $5\le n\le60$, **53-TET has the smallest weighted RMS**: fifth error $0.068$ ¢, major-third error $1.408$ ¢, weighted RMS $1.495$ ¢. 31-TET has a very accurate major third; 19-TET keeps both the fifth and major-third errors around $7$ ¢; 24-TET improves neither the fifth nor the major third, because 24 is a multiple of 12 and the nearest grid points for these two targets in 12-TET are retained. "Optimal" here is responsible only to this metric; it does not stand for all musical goals.

The cost is equally direct: 53 degrees per octave significantly increases the complexity of keyboards, fingering, and score reading. Smaller mathematical error does not mean engineering usability rises with it; all $n$-TET systems share the same grid-transposition symmetry, and 12-TET's advantage is fewer degrees while already approximating the fifth and other targets well.

### 4.4.2 Continued Fractions: Why Always 12, 41, 53

These numbers are not a coincidence. The position of the perfect fifth in semitone coordinates is

$$
\log_2 \frac{3}{2} \approx 0.5849625,
$$

and its fractional part can be expanded as a continued fraction. Its convergents are successively

$$
\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{3}{5},\ \frac{7}{12},\ \frac{24}{41},\ \frac{31}{53}, \dots
$$

The denominators $12, 41, 53$ are exactly the best answers to "how many equal divisions to use to approximate the perfect fifth." $7/12$ gives $700$ ¢ (error $1.955$ ¢); $31/53$ gives $701.887$ ¢ (error $0.068$ ¢). Continued fractions guarantee that, among all tunings with $n$ not exceeding a given bound, these denominators give the optimal approximation to the fifth. The appearance of $41, 53$ among the top five in Table 4 of Experiment 4.1 is exactly this number-theoretic structure at work.

### 4.4.3 Changing the Equivalence Relation: 22 Shruti and Bohlen–Pierce

All the discussion above assumes octave equivalence. But that assumption can be changed:

- **22 shruti**: the Indian classical tradition divides the octave into 22 "microtones," about $54.545$ ¢ each. It is not an equal division, and the 22 notes differ somewhat across sources, but it shows that "the number of degrees in an octave" need not be 12;
- **Bohlen–Pierce**: takes $3:1$ (the **tritave**, $1901.955$ ¢) as the equivalence unit and divides it into 13 equal parts, $1901.955/13 = 146.304$ ¢ each. It has no octave equivalence, and the scale sounds "unlike any traditional music," but it is a self-consistent mathematical system.

These two examples remind us: octave equivalence in Chapter 2 is a **postulate**, not a theorem. Replace it, and pitch geometry becomes a different circle (circumference $1901.955$ instead of $1200$), and all of "consonance," "scale," and "transposition" must be redefined.

## 4.5 Summary

This chapter wrote tuning as a constrained optimization problem:

$$
\underbrace{\text{Objective:}\min \sum_i w_i e_i^2}_{\text{consonance}}
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\underbrace{n \text{ equal degrees} + \text{transposition invariance}}_{\text{engineering constraints}}
$$

- **Pythagorean tuning**: 12 perfect fifths need a $23.460$ ¢ gap to close (Theorem 4.1), and the gap becomes the wolf fifth $678.495$ ¢. It is simple and ancient, but uneven.
- **Just intonation**: locally optimal for a fixed tonic (major third $5:4$; its only error is $-13.686$ ¢ relative to 12-TET), but three pure major thirds need a $41.059$ ¢ gap to fill an octave, and it collapses on transposition.
- **12-TET**: the only $n=12$ scheme satisfying "equal division + transposition invariance"; the price is that, apart from the octave, all intervals are irrational (Theorem 4.2). Fifth error $1.955$ ¢, major-third error $13.686$ ¢.
- **n-TET**: after relaxing $n$, 53-TET's weighted RMS is only $1.495$ ¢ and 31-TET's is $5.695$ ¢; but engineering-wise 12 is still the winner. The continued fractions $7/12, 24/41, 31/53$ explain why good $n$ always appears at these denominators.

In one sentence: **tuning is not a contest of "who is more accurate" but a budget allocation of "where to put the error."** 12-TET moves error from the fifth to the major third, trading an audible third error for global transposition freedom; this is not a mathematical optimum but an engineering Pareto optimum.

The next chapter accepts the 12-TET grid and searches for scales on it: choose 7 of the 12 pitch classes, enumerate all $C(12,7) = 792$ possibilities, and see why the major scale wins.
