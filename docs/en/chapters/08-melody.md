# Chapter 8 · Melody: Statistics and Information Theory

<div class="chapter-meta">
  <span>Estimated 45 minutes</span>
  <span>Prerequisite: Chapters 0, 2, and 7</span>
  <span>Code: code/ch08_melody.py</span>
</div>

This chapter answers five questions:

1. Why does a melody sound like "a piece" rather than randomly drawn notes?
2. Looking only at the previous note, what is the probability of guessing the next one?
3. What is entropy, and why does it measure both "information content" and "unpredictability"?
4. Can "unexpected yet inevitable" be written as a computable quantity?
5. Why does a melody generated by a program from statistical regularities quickly spin in place?

Chapter 7 cut time into cells and described rhythm with integers and remainders. This chapter moves to another axis: **pitch sequences**. Mathematically the two are the same routine—discretize first, then ask "to what degree can the next step be guessed". A scale cuts continuous frequency into seven or twelve letters, and a melody is a string of those letters; the remaining question is: how much regularity is in this string? Can the regularity be written as probabilities? And once written down, can it conversely generate new strings?

Put the conclusion up front so the reader does not get lost in formulas:

$$
\underbrace{P_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_i}}_{\text{style = conditional distribution}}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\underbrace{H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i}_{\text{average information}}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\underbrace{s(x) = -\log_2 p(x)}_{\text{how surprising this step is}}
$$

The three objects are a **transition matrix**, **entropy**, and **surprise**. The first describes "how the next note follows in this style", the second "how hard it is to guess on average", and the third "how surprising a particular step is". The experiment in this chapter computes all three, uses them to generate a melody, and then honestly points out where it fails.

## 8.1 Melody as a Markov Chain: Transition Probabilities and Style

### 8.1.1 From Scale to Alphabet: Writing a Melody as a Sequence

Chapter 2 wrote pitch in cent coordinates, and Chapter 5 cut the 12 pitch classes into scales. At the melody level, the laziest approach is to keep only **pitch classes** and project away octave, duration, dynamics, and timbre. A melody then degenerates into a sequence over an alphabet.

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.1 (melody, alphabet, and context)">

Let the alphabet be $S=\{C,D,E,F,G,A,B\}$ (numbered $0,1,\ldots,6$), with $K=\lvert S\rvert=7$. A **melody** is a finite sequence
$$
x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N \in S^N .
$$
The **$k$-th order context** of $x_t$ is the $k$ notes before it, $(x_{t-k},\ldots,x_{t-1})$. This chapter discusses only $k=1$ and $k=2$.

</Box>

Why not simply use the 12 semitones as the alphabet? A quick calculation shows why: 12 states give $12\times12=144$ transition cells, but the corpus has only 47 transitions, filling less than a third ($47/144=0.326$); fewer than half a sample per cell on average. Seven pitch classes cut the state count to 7; among 48 notes each pitch class appears $48/7\approx 6.9$ times on average, barely enough to estimate. **Every doubling of the state count roughly doubles the sample size needed**—this is the first practical constraint of any statistical model, harder than any formula.

Table 1 is this 12-bar corpus. The left column is note names, the middle is their pitch-class coordinates in 12-TET (semitones relative to C), and the right is the semitone difference between adjacent notes within the bar. There are 48 notes and 47 adjacent intervals: 29 ascending, 18 descending, 0 repeated; 36 are stepwise (semitone difference at most 2), a share of $0.7660$.

These three statistics already reveal the melody's "character": no repeated notes, meaning adjacent notes are never the same, so the diagonal of the transition matrix will be all $0$; steps account for $76.6\%$, meaning leaps are the exception rather than the rule. In real repertoire these two proportions differ, but they **determine the shape of the matrix**—an all-zero diagonal means the option "repeat" does not exist in the model at all.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: this corpus represents some real style">

No. It is fixed text handwritten by the author, with one purpose: to make every number reproducible and hand-checkable. It certainly resembles a little piece in C major, but it was not compiled from any score, and the sample is only 48 notes. Every conclusion in this chapter is a conclusion "on this 48-note corpus"; change the corpus and the matrix changes, the entropy changes, and the generated melody changes. Treating probabilities estimated from 48 notes as "the essence of music" is treating a thermometer as the climate.

</Box>

### 8.1.2 First-Order Chains and Transition Matrices

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.2 (first-order Markov chain and transition matrix)">

If for every $t$
$$
p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \ldots, x_1) = p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}),
$$
the sequence is said to follow a **first-order Markov chain**. Its **transition matrix** $P=(P_{ij})$ is defined by
$$
P_{ij} = p(x_t = j \mid x_{t-1} = i),\qquad i,j\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\},
$$
with nonnegative entries and row sums $1$ (a row-stochastic matrix). Estimating from a corpus uses maximum likelihood:
$$
\hat P_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_i},\qquad n_{ij}=\#\{t: x_{t-1}=i,\ x_t=j\},\quad n_i=\sum_j n_{ij}.
$$

</Box>

The tone of this definition deserves attention: it does not say "music really is a Markov chain"; it says "assume it is for now, and see how much predictability can be squeezed out". The assumption itself is a model, not a fact; the hold-out test in Section 8.2 is there to test the assumption.

<Box kind="algorithm" title="Algorithm 8.1 (estimate a first-order transition matrix from a corpus)">

```text
输入：旋律 x_1..x_N，字母表大小 K，平滑常数 α
1.  n[i][j] ← 0（7×7 全零）
2.  for t = 2 .. N:
        n[x_{t-1}][x_t] ← n[x_{t-1}][x_t] + 1
3.  for i = 0 .. K-1:
        s_i ← Σ_j n[i][j]
        P[i][j] ← (n[i][j] + α) / (s_i + αK)
输出：P
```
$\alpha=0$ is maximum likelihood; $\alpha=1$ is Laplace smoothing, which guarantees a nonzero probability even for continuations never observed. Tables 2 and 3 use $\alpha=0$.

</Box>

Table 2 is the count matrix and Table 3 the row-normalized probability matrix. Three rows are worth reading separately:

- **Row C**: $n_{C}=6$, and all six counts fall on D, so $\hat P_{C\to D}=1.000$. In this corpus, C is **always** followed by D.
- **Row D**: $n_D=9$, with 7 going to E, 1 to C, and 1 to F, so the row probabilities are $(0.111,\ 0.778,\ 0.111)$—more than one and a half of the three steps are the stepwise motion D to E.
- **Row E**: $n_E=12$, the row with the most samples; its four successors take $0.333$, $0.167$, $0.417$, and $0.083$. More samples make the row "thicker" and the distribution closer to genuine uncertainty.

Conversely, row B has only 1 sample (after B only G ever occurs), and maximum likelihood gives $\hat P_{B\to G}=1.000$. This "certainty" rests on a single sample and is completely untrustworthy; Laplace smoothing would compress it to something like $(1+1)/(1+7)=0.250$. **Cells with probability $0$ or $1$ are often not regularities but insufficient samples.**

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: probability 1 means 'certain'">

$0$ and $1$ are boundary values of maximum likelihood on finite samples. Six samples all going to D only says "no exception among these 6", not that the true probability is 1. The Bayesian statement is more accurate: the samples give a posterior distribution, and $\hat p=1$ is only the posterior mode, with a very wide confidence interval. This chapter stays with maximum likelihood so that every number can be hand-computed; keep one thing in mind: for every $0.000$ and $1.000$, ask "how many samples support this?"

</Box>

<Figure src="/figures/transition-heatmap.svg" caption="Heat map of the first-order transition matrix: rows are the current pitch class, columns the next. Blank cells are not 'rules with probability zero' but combinations absent from the corpus—only 48 notes." />

<Box kind="algorithm" title="Algorithm 8.2 (sample a melody by temperature)">

```text
输入：P，长度 T，温度 τ，起始音 x_0，随机种子
1.  Q[i][j] ← P[i][j]^(1/τ) / Σ_j P[i][j]^(1/τ)    （行内归一化）
2.  for t = 1 .. T:
        u ← Uniform(0,1)
        在 Q[x_{t-1}] 的累积分布里找第一个超过 u 的音 j
        x_t ← j
3.  输出 x_0..x_T 与每一步的 p = Q[x_{t-1}][x_t]
```
At $\tau=1$, $Q=P$; $\tau \lt 1$ sharpens the distribution (more conservative), and $\tau \gt 1$ flattens it (more adventurous).

</Box>

### 8.1.3 Second-Order Context and the Cost of Sparsity

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.3 (second-order chain and context)">

If $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1},x_{t-2},\ldots)=p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_{t-2})$, the sequence is said to follow a **second-order Markov chain**. Its parameters are grouped by context $(i,j)$:
$$
p(x_t = c \mid x_{t-1}=i,\ x_{t-2}=j) = P^{(2)}_{ij,c},
$$
There are $K^2=49$ contexts. Each context corresponds to a row of 7 probabilities summing to $1$, so there are $49\times(7-1)=294$ free parameters.

</Box>

294 free parameters, 46 second-order samples—fewer than $0.16$ samples per parameter on average. Table 5 shows the actual situation: only 17 of the 49 contexts are observed, the other 32 never occur; the most frequent context, D→E, appears only 7 times, with successors C:2, F:4, G:1.

At this point "conditional entropy $0.7644$ bit is less than the first-order $1.2167$ bit" looks beautiful, but it is a lie. 46 samples spread over 17 contexts average $2.7$ per context; any context that occurs only once has conditional entropy $0$ (a unique successor). Entropy estimates that plug in frequencies directly are systematically biased downward by this "false certainty". Section 8.2.3 uses a hold-out test to tear off this fig leaf.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: higher order means a more accurate model">

Higher order necessarily lowers in-sample entropy—this is not a victory of the model but the definition of overfitting. More parameters give the model more ability to memorize noise as regularity. The right way to judge a model is cross-entropy on **unseen data**, not entropy on the training set. Table 6 gives the counterexample: on 24 test notes, the second-order model's cross-entropy $2.2758$ loses to the first-order model's $2.1741$.

</Box>

## 8.2 Music Information Theory: Entropy, Redundancy, and Predictability

### 8.2.1 Entropy: How Many Bits on Average to State the Next Note

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.4 (Shannon entropy)">

Let a random variable $X$ take $K$ symbols with probabilities $p_0,\ldots,p_{K-1}$. Its **entropy** is
$$
H(X) = -\sum_{i=0}^{K-1} p_i \log_2 p_i ,
$$
in bits, with the convention $0\log_2 0 = 0$.

</Box>

The intuition for entropy is "average surprise": if a symbol has probability $p$, the surprise needed to state it is $-\log_2 p$ bits; entropy is this quantity averaged with weights $p$. A symbol with probability 1 contributes $0$ bits (no need to guess), and a symbol with probability $1/K$ contributes $\log_2 K$ bits.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 8.1 (bounds and maximum of entropy)">

For any distribution on $K$ symbols,
$$
0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 K .
$$
Equality on the right holds if and only if the distribution is uniform, $p_i = 1/K$.

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

Write $x_i = 1/p_i$. By the concavity of $\log_2$ and Jensen's inequality (weights $p_i$, $\sum_i p_i = 1$):
$$
H(X) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{1}{p_i}
\le \log_2 \sum_i p_i \cdot \frac{1}{p_i}
= \log_2 K .
$$
Jensen's inequality is an equality if and only if all $x_i$ are equal, that is, all $p_i$ are equal. Nonnegativity follows directly from $-p_i\log_2 p_i \ge 0$ for each term. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

On the 48-note corpus, the marginal distribution is C $0.1250$, D $0.1875$, E $0.2500$, F $0.1667$, G $0.1667$, A $0.0833$, B $0.0208$, and the marginal entropy is
$$
H(X) = 2.6046\ \text{bit},
$$
while the upper bound is $\log_2 7 = 2.8074$ bits. The two differ by only $0.2028$ bit. That is, **looking at a single note, the seven letters are spread quite evenly and are almost impossible to guess**. Uniform does not mean boring—uniform precisely means "no note is the default answer", so predictability must be sought in context.

### 8.2.2 Conditional Entropy and Redundancy

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.5 (joint and conditional entropy)">

For a joint distribution $p(x,y)$,
$$
H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log_2 p(x,y),
\qquad
H(X\mid Y) = H(X,Y)-H(Y).
$$
Equivalently, $H(X\mid Y)=\sum_y p(y) H(X\mid Y=y)$, that is, "group by $y$ first, then take the weighted average of the within-group entropies".

</Box>

Taking $Y$ as "the previous note" and $X$ as "the current note" gives the first-order conditional entropy
$$
H_1 = H(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N-1} H\bigl(P_{i\cdot}\bigr),
$$
where $H(P_{i\cdot})$ is the entropy of row $i$ of the transition matrix. Substituting the experimental data gives $H_1 = 1.2167$ bits.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 8.2 (conditioning cannot increase entropy)">

For any joint distribution,
$$
H(X\mid Y) \le H(X),
$$
with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

First show that entropy $H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i$ is a concave function of $p$: each term $f(t)=-t\log_2 t$ has second derivative $-1/(t\ln 2)\lt 0$ on $[0,1]$, so $f$ is concave; a sum of concave functions is concave.

Then
$$
H(X\mid Y) = \sum_y p(y) H\bigl(p(\cdot\mid y)\bigr)
\le H\Bigl(\sum_y p(y)\, p(\cdot\mid y)\Bigr)
= H\bigl(p(\cdot)\bigr) = H(X),
$$
where the middle $\le$ is exactly concavity (Jensen) applied to the weighted average of the probability vectors $\{p(\cdot\mid y)\}$ with weights $p(y)$. Equality holds if and only if all conditional distributions $p(\cdot\mid y)$ are the same, that is, $X$ and $Y$ are independent. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

Putting the two numbers together lets us define the most central ratio of this chapter.

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.6 (redundancy)">

Let $H$ be the entropy rate of the model in use (this chapter takes the conditional entropy $H_1$), and $H_{\max}=\log_2 K$. Call
$$
R = 1 - \frac{H}{H_{\max}}
$$
the **redundancy**. $R=0$ means completely unpredictable, $R=1$ completely determined.

</Box>

Substituting the data:
$$
R = 1 - \frac{1.2167}{2.8074} = 0.5666 .
$$
Using marginal entropy instead gives $1-2.6046/2.8074 = 0.0722$, an order of magnitude apart. **The choice of denominator determines what redundancy means**: $0.5666$ relative to $H_{\max}$ says "how far from maximum entropy", while the compression rate relative to marginal entropy, $\frac{2.6046-1.2167}{2.6046}=53.3\%$, says "how much uncertainty context actually removed". If a paper does not state which one it uses, the numbers cannot be compared.

Redundancy is not a defect. **Redundancy is predictability**, and predictability is another name for "style": someone familiar with a style can predict the next note in the middle of a phrase. Shannon's 1951 guessing experiment on English gave character-entropy estimates between $0.6$ and $1.3$ bits, far below the upper bound $\log_2 26\approx4.7$—natural language is more than half redundant, and so is music. A sequence with no redundancy at all ($R=0$) sounds like noise: every note is equally surprising, so no note is "due".

### 8.2.3 Estimation Bias and the Hold-Out Test

The suspense left in Section 8.1.3 must be resolved here. Entropy obtained by plugging in frequencies is a **biased** estimate: it treats "not observed" as "impossible", so it systematically underestimates the true value. The fewer the samples and the more the cells, the worse the underestimate.

A common correction is Miller–Madow:
$$
\hat H_{\text{MM}} = \hat H + \frac{m-1}{2N\ln 2},
$$
where $N$ is the number of samples and $m$ the number of actually nonempty cells. For marginal entropy the formula applies directly: nonempty cells $m=7$, $N=48$, correction $0.0901$, raising $2.6046$ to $2.6947$ bits.

Conditional entropy is estimated by grouping by predecessor, so the whole joint table cannot be treated as one multinomial. Weighting each row's correction by the row's sample count turns the total increment into $(m-r)/(2N\ln 2)$, where $m=18$ is the number of nonzero joint cells, $r=7$ the number of nonempty predecessor rows, and $N=47$ the number of transition pairs. The correction is $11/(2\times47\times0.6931)=0.1688$, raising $\hat H_1$ from $1.2167$ to $1.3855$ bits. If one mistakenly uses $(m-1)/(2N\ln2)$, one gets $1.4776$ bits—that is the wrong accounting, treating joint cells as a single multinomial.

The correction is only a stopgap. The real test is **hold-out**: split the corpus in half, train on the first 6 bars (24 notes), test on the last 6 bars (24 notes), and score the test notes with the model:
$$
\text{CE} = -\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t\in\text{test}} \log_2 \hat p\bigl(x_t \mid \text{context}_t\bigr).
$$
This quantity is **cross-entropy**, in bits per note, meaning "using this model to predict unseen data, how many bits per step on average". The better the model, the lower the cross-entropy. Laplace smoothing uses $\alpha=1$; the second-order model backs off to first order when it meets an unseen context.

The results in Table 6 are clear:

| Model | Cross-entropy (bit/note) | Hit rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0th order (marginal only) | 2.6655 | 0.2500 (6/24) |
| 1st order | 2.1741 | 0.5000 (12/24) |
| 2nd order | 2.2758 | 0.4583 (11/24) |

The first-order model compresses cross-entropy from $2.6655$ to $2.1741$ and raises the hit rate from $25\%$ to $50\%$—looking only at the previous note, it guesses half correctly. The second-order model instead regresses: $2.2758$, $45.8\%$. The reason is not that second order is "useless" but that **there are not enough samples**: 24 training notes fill only a tiny fraction of the 49 contexts, most contexts must back off to first order, and the backoff process brings in the smoothing bias. The number $17/49$ in Table 5 is the preview of this failure.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: lower in-sample entropy means a better model">

The second-order entropy in Table 5, $0.7644$, is $0.4523$ bit below the first-order $1.2167$; looking only at this number would lead to "second order is better". The hold-out test in Table 6 gives the opposite answer. Low entropy on the training set only proves that "the model memorized the data", not that "the model understood the regularity". The criterion is always: **how many bits does it spend on unseen data?**

</Box>

## 8.3 Why Good Music Is "Unexpected Yet Inevitable"

### 8.3.1 Surprise and Shannon Guessing

<Box kind="definition" title="Definition 8.7 (surprise and guess rank)">

Given a probability $p(x)$ under a model, the **surprise** of event $x$ is
$$
s(x) = -\log_2 p(x)\quad(\text{bit}),
$$
with the convention $s=0$ when $p=1$. Sort the successor notes by decreasing probability and call the position of the true successor in this list (starting from 1) its **rank** $r(x)$.

</Box>

$s(x)$ measures "how surprising this step is", and $r(x)$ measures "how many guesses it takes". They are not the same quantity, but a clean inequality connects them.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 8.4 (surprise is at least the logarithm of the rank)">

If candidates are ordered by decreasing probability and $x$ has rank $r$, then
$$
-\log_2 p(x) \ge \log_2 r .
$$

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

Rank $r$ means the $r-1$ candidates ahead of it all have probability at least $p(x)$ (ordered by decreasing probability, ties allowed). Hence
$$
1 = \sum_{i} p_i \ge \sum_{i=1}^{r} p_i \ge r\,p(x),
$$
so $p(x) \le 1/r$. Taking $-\log_2$ on both sides (a decreasing function) gives $-\log_2 p(x) \ge \log_2 r$. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

Taking expectations gives "average surprise is at least the average $\log_2$ rank". This inequality is the theoretical basis of Shannon's guessing experiment: **the rank needed to guess correctly is a lower-bound proxy for entropy**. In 1951 Shannon had subjects guess the next letter of English and used the average rank of a correct guess to estimate the entropy of English, relying on exactly this relation.

The "subject" in this chapter is a perfect statistician: it knows the whole transition matrix and guesses in decreasing order of probability. The experimental data (Table 7):

- mean rank $E[r] = 1.5532$—on average one and a half guesses suffice;
- top-1 hit rate $0.6170$ ($29/47$), top-2 $0.8511$ ($40/47$), top-3 $0.9787$ ($46/47$);
- rank distribution $1:29,\ 2:11,\ 3:6,\ 4:1$, and nothing beyond fifth;
- mean surprise $E[s] = 1.2167$ bits, exactly equal to the first-order conditional entropy of Table 4.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Theorem 8.3 (mean surprise of a first-order chain equals its conditional entropy)">

Let the transition matrix of a first-order chain be $\hat P_{ij}=n_{ij}/n_i$. Scoring the corpus's 47 transitions with it gives mean surprise
$$
\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{t=2}^{N} \Bigl(-\log_2 \hat P_{x_{t-1},x_t}\Bigr) = H_1 .
$$

</Box>

<Box kind="proof" title="Proof">

Group the sum by predecessor and use $n_{ij}=\hat P_{ij}\,n_i$:
$$
\sum_{t=2}^{N}\Bigl(-\log_2 \hat P_{x_{t-1},x_t}\Bigr)
= \sum_{i}\sum_{j} n_{ij}\bigl(-\log_2 \hat P_{ij}\bigr)
= \sum_i n_i \sum_j \hat P_{ij}\bigl(-\log_2 \hat P_{ij}\bigr)
= \sum_i n_i\, H(P_{i\cdot}).
$$
Dividing both sides by $N-1$, the right side is exactly the definition of $H_1$. $\blacksquare$

</Box>

Surprise is "entropy per event", and entropy is "the average of surprise". Two sides of the same coin: the model's entropy rate gives the **expected** bits per step, and the corpus's actual average happens to land exactly on that expectation—an inevitable consequence of self-evaluation on the same data, neither coincidence nor validation. Real validation looks at unseen new data (the cross-entropy of Section 8.2.3).

Note that the mean $\log_2$ rank is only $0.4789$ bit, far below $E[s]=1.2167$ bits. Theorem 8.4 guarantees "not less than", not "close to": the more skewed the distribution, the harder rank compression. A note with probability $0.5$ and one with probability $0.083$ may differ by only 1 in rank, yet differ by $2.585$ bits in surprise. **Rank is a coarse ruler; entropy is fine-grained.**

### 8.3.2 Temperature: Tuning Between Entropy and Predictability

The temperature $\tau$ in Algorithm 8.2 is the only "knob" in this chapter:
$$
Q_{ij}(\tau) = \frac{P_{ij}^{1/\tau}}{\sum_k P_{ik}^{1/\tau}} .
$$
$\tau=1$ restores the corpus; $\tau\lt1$ magnifies large probabilities and suppresses small ones, sharpening the distribution; $\tau\gt1$ flattens it.

Table 8 gives three settings:

| $\tau$ | Expected surprise | Mean surprise of 32 generated notes | Distinct pitch classes | Mean interval (semitones) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 0.9787 | 0.9674 | 6 | 2.097 |
| 1.0 | 1.2580 | 1.5417 | 5 | 2.129 |
| 2.0 | 1.3958 | 1.4963 | 5 | 2.129 |

"Expected surprise" is the expectation of $-\log_2 p$ under the stationary distribution, that is, $\sum_i \pi_i H(Q_{i\cdot})$, where $\pi$ is the chain's stationary distribution. At $T=1$, $\pi$ is C $0.099$, D $0.162$, E $0.244$, F $0.173$, G $0.187$, A $0.107$, B $0.027$—E accounts for nearly a quarter because it has the most outgoing edges and is easiest to reach.

As $\tau$ rises from $0.5$ to $2.0$, expected surprise rises from $0.9787$ to $1.3958$, an increase of $42.6\%$. But **it is still far from the upper bound**: $\log_2 7 = 2.8074$, while $\tau=2$ gives only $1.3958$. The reason lies in the support: temperature can only redistribute probability over existing transitions, not invent new continuations. Each row has at most 4 nonzero entries (row E), so even if $\tau\to\infty$ flattens the row, the row entropy is capped at $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: temperature is a 'creativity knob'">

Temperature is not creativity. It generates no new notes, changes no alphabet, and introduces no continuation absent from the corpus; it only redistributes existing probabilities. A melody with "more imagination" requires changing the model (longer context, motivic transformation, harmonic constraints, a rhythm layer), not turning $\tau$ up. Turn it to $\tau=10$ and you do not get creativity, only an approximation of uniform noise.

</Box>

Table 8 contains another detail worth mentioning: at $\tau=1.0$, the empirical mean surprise of the 32 generated notes, $1.5417$, is higher than the stationary expectation $1.2580$ by $0.2837$ bit. This is not the model's fault but **sample size's** fault: the standard deviation of 31 independent samples is on the order of $0.2$ bits, and another seed would make this number jump up or down.

### 8.3.3 The Generated Melody, and Why It Spins in Place

Table 9 is an 8-bar melody generated with $\tau=1.0$ and seed 8:

```text
1  C D F E     2  F D E G     3  E F E D     4  E C D C
5  D C D E     6  F E C D     7  F D E D     8  E D E D
```

There are 31 generated steps, total surprise $47.7914$ bits, mean $1.5417$ bit/note; only 5 pitch classes occur (C, D, E, F, G), while A and B never appear; the sum of absolute semitone intervals is 66, mean $2.129$ semitones.

Reading the surprises one by one reveals two things. First, **5 steps have surprise exactly $0$** (steps 1, 14, 16, 18, 23): all are C→D, because in the corpus C is only followed by D, and the model has no alternative. These steps are pure copying and carry no information. Second, **the largest step is step 7, E→G, at $3.5850$ bits**, because E→G occurs only once in the corpus ($p=0.0833$).

Then comes the failure. Steps 13 through 18 are C D C D C D—six notes alternating between C and D. The reason is mechanical: $P_{C\to D}=1$ and $P_{D\to C}=0.111$, so once the chain reaches C it is locked into a two-note loop and can escape only by the $0.111$ chance. The last two generated bars (bar 7's F D E D and bar 8's E D E D) similarly oscillate between D and E.

This is not the random seed's fault but the **model structure's** fault. A first-order chain has only one memory: which note came last. It does not know "which bar we are in", "where this phrase should close", or "repeating this note three times will sound cheap". Its entire worldview is a $7\times7$ table. Expecting it to write a melody with syntax, contour, and cadence is like expecting a parrot that only completes sentences to write a novel.

<Box kind="pitfall" title="Common misconception: a statistical model automatically learns 'musicality'">

A statistical model learns **conditional distributions**, not **structure**. The chain in this chapter can reproduce local habits such as "C is followed by D" and "D mostly goes to E", but it cannot express global constraints such as "bar 4 should return to the tonic", "this passage is a parallel phrase", or "the climax is in the penultimate phrase". At least three layers of musicality are untouched by the model: rhythm and syntax (Chapter 7 only did cells), harmonic progression (Chapter 6 is another coordinate system), and listener expectation (a psychological empirical fact, not a corpus statistic). Only with these three layers could one talk about "generating music"; adding only probabilities yields a parrot that sounds more like the corpus.

</Box>

So can "unexpected yet inevitable" be written as a formula? Roughly, yes. Denote the surprise sequence of a melody by $s_1,\ldots,s_T$; a good melody roughly satisfies two conditions at once:

1. **The mean falls in a middle range**: an $\bar s$ that is too low (near $0$) is recitation, and one that is too high (near $\log_2 K$) is noise;
2. **The sequence has structure**: local peaks (leaps, turns, surprising notes) with valleys between them (steps, repeated motifs, predictable transitions), rather than $0$ and $3.17$ alternating at random.

The generated melody of Table 9 satisfies the first (with $1.5417$ near half of $2.8074$) but completely fails the second: its surprise sequence has no "phrase" shape at all, only row-by-row sampling from a probability table. **"Inevitable" is predictability (low conditional entropy), "unexpected" is local surprise peaks; a good melody is the temporal structure of the two, not their average.**

## 8.4 Summary

This chapter built a chain among three objects:

$$
\underbrace{P_{ij}=\frac{n_{ij}}{n_i}}_{\text{style}}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\underbrace{H=-\sum p\log_2 p}_{\text{average information}}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\underbrace{s(x)=-\log_2 p(x)}_{\text{surprise of this step}}
$$

Three main conclusions:

- **A melody's "style" can be written as a conditional distribution**. The 48-note corpus gives 47 transitions, from which a $7\times7$ matrix is estimated; looking only at the previous note, the hit rate on the test set is $0.5000$ and the cross-entropy $2.1741$ bit/note. But the same corpus covers only $17/49$ second-order contexts, and the second-order model loses to the first-order one in the hold-out test ($2.2758$ versus $2.1741$).
- **Entropy and redundancy measure "predictability", and the denominator matters**. Marginal entropy $H(X)=2.6046$ bits, first-order conditional entropy $H_1=1.2167$ bits, upper bound $\log_2 7=2.8074$ bits; redundancy relative to the upper bound is $R=0.5666$, while conditioning compresses $53.3\%$ relative to marginal entropy. These two percentages answer different questions, and the denominator must be stated when citing them.
- **Surprise turns "unexpectedness" into additive bits**. The corpus's mean surprise equals $H_1=1.2167$ bits; guessing in decreasing probability gives mean rank $1.5532$, with $61.70\%$ hit at one guess and $97.87\%$ within three; Theorem 8.4 guarantees $-\log_2 p\ge\log_2 r$. Temperature $\tau$ can move expected surprise from $0.9787$ to $1.3958$ bits, but the upper bound is pinned at $\log_2 4=2$ bits by each row's support.

The failure boundaries must be stated clearly: this chapter compresses melody into a sequence over seven pitch classes, discarding octave, duration, dynamics, timbre, harmony, and syntax; it replaces "listener expectation" with "corpus statistics", which holds only approximately when the listener knows the style; the 48-note sample supports only a first-order model, and any finer partition (12 semitones, second order, third order) runs into sparsity. These are not implementation details but the foundation of the model.

Back to the five opening questions: a melody sounds like "a piece" because the conditional distribution of adjacent notes is stable and listeners can predict; looking only at the previous note guesses half correctly ($0.5000$), and within three guesses $97.87\%$; entropy is average information and at the same time "how hard it is to guess on average"; "unexpected yet inevitable" is roughly "low conditional entropy plus a surprise sequence with peak structure", but it is a temporal structure, not a scalar; the program's melody spins in place because a first-order chain has one cell of memory, and the certainty of C→D locks it into a two-note loop.
